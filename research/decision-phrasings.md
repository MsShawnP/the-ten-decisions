# Ten Decisions — CEO-Language Phrasings

**Purpose:** Section headers and thematic anchors for the manifesto and exec summary.
**Test applied:** "Does this sound like something a CEO would say at their kitchen table at 6am — not in a board deck?"
**Source:** Brainstorm phrasings from `docs/brainstorms/ten-decisions-requirements.md`

---

## Arc 1 — What You Have Today (Decisions 1–5)

### Decision 1 — SKU Rationalization

**Final phrasing:** "Which SKUs should we keep, kill, or double down on?"

**Status:** KEPT — no revision needed.

**Rationale:** "Keep, kill, or double down" has direct kitchen-table energy. A CEO absolutely says this out loud. The three-part construction also works cleanly as a manifesto header.

---

### Decision 2 — Product Data Health

**Final phrasing:** "Will our product data hold up when the next retailer asks for it?"

**Revised from:** "Will our product data survive the next retailer onboarding?"

**Rationale:** "Retailer onboarding" is an internal/operational phrase that a CEO might use but rarely says with urgency at 6am. "Hold up when the next retailer asks for it" places the CEO in the moment of truth — the call, not the process. Keeps "will it hold up" as the fear and "when the next retailer asks" as the trigger.

---

### Decision 3 — Deduction Recovery

**Final phrasing:** "How much of what we billed last quarter was quietly taken back — and did we fight it?"

**Revised from:** "How much of our top-line revenue is being clawed back — and can we fight it?"

**Rationale:** "Top-line revenue" is finance-speak; no CEO says "top-line revenue" at the kitchen table. "What we billed last quarter" is concrete and personal. Changing "can we fight it" (a capability question) to "did we fight it" (a past-tense accountability question) adds a sharper edge — it implies the company may already have let it go.

---

### Decision 4 — Fulfillment Reliability

**Final phrasing:** "Are we losing repeat orders because we can't ship what we've promised?"

**Revised from:** "Are operational bottlenecks quietly killing our repeat retailer orders?"

**Rationale:** "Operational bottlenecks" is one of the purest examples of consultant jargon — no CEO says this at the kitchen table. The revised version names the actual fear (losing repeat orders) and the actual failure mode (can't ship what we promised). "Promised" carries the emotional weight of a commitment that wasn't kept.

---

### Decision 5 — EDI Compliance

**Final phrasing:** "Are we paying chargebacks we could have caught before the truck left the dock?"

**Revised from:** "Is our EDI creating or preventing chargebacks?"

**Rationale:** The original is slightly abstract — "creating or preventing" is a binary evaluation, not a kitchen-table feeling. The revised version gives the CEO the exact moment of frustration: paying for errors that a pre-flight check would have caught before shipment. "Truck left the dock" is specific and visceral. Note: "EDI" was deliberately removed from the header — the section body will define what EDI is, but the header question captures the pain without requiring the reader to know the acronym.

## Arc 2 — Seeing Clearly (Decisions 6–7)

### Decision 6 — Channel Profitability

**Final phrasing:** "Which channels actually make us money?"

**Status:** KEPT — no revision needed.

**Rationale:** Perfect as-is. Short, direct, first-person plural. "Actually" carries the implication that the company may not currently know — which is the whole point. The shortest phrasings are often the strongest ones.

---

### Decision 7 — Revenue Lifecycle

**Final phrasing:** "Why does our bank account look empty when our sales pipeline looks full?"

**Status:** KEPT — no revision needed.

**Rationale:** The best phrasing in the original brief. The contrast between "bank account" and "sales pipeline" captures the exact cognitive dissonance every specialty food CEO has felt. This is the sentence a founder says out loud to their co-founder at the kitchen table. Do not touch it.

---

## Arc 3 — What's Next (Decisions 8–9)

### Decision 8 — Retail Readiness

**Final phrasing:** "What breaks first when a major retailer finally says yes to us?"

**Revised from:** "Are we ready if the next major retailer says yes?"

**Rationale:** "Are we ready if" is a hedged, passive question — it lets the reader say "yes, we're ready" and move on. The revised version assumes the retailer WILL say yes ("finally says yes to us" — the word "finally" implies they've been working toward this) and forces the CEO to confront the failure mode: what breaks. This is the 6am thought, not the board meeting answer.

---

### Decision 9 — Launch Economics

**Final phrasing:** "Do we actually know what saying yes to this retailer will cost us in Year 1?"

**Revised from:** "What does that retailer actually cost us in the first 12 months?"

**Rationale:** The original asks "what does it cost" — which assumes the CEO already did the math and is just checking the number. The revised version adds "do we actually know" — which challenges whether the math was ever done. This is the more honest and more important question for a brand that just got an inbound from a major retailer. "This retailer" (specific, present) is more urgent than "that retailer" (generic, hypothetical).

---

## Arc 4 — Discipline (Decision 10)

### Decision 10 — Weekly Pulse

**Final phrasing:** "What are the three numbers I need to see Monday morning?"

**Status:** KEPT — no revision needed.

**Rationale:** Perfect as-is. First-person, specific (Monday morning, three numbers), and carries the implication that the answer to every other decision in this manifesto ultimately comes back to whether you're watching the right numbers every week. This is the closer — the question that ties the piece together.
